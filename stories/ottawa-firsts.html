Ottawa Firsts

Many things that are part of our day-to-day lives had their origin at one point or another. Let's take a look at some of the firsts for the city.

Traffic Lights

At 8 a.m. on March 5, 1928, pedestrians, motorists and streetcar motormen saw the first automatic traffic lights in Ottawa. The lights were installed along Sparks Street at the corners of Metcalfe, O’Connor and Bank Streets. A police officer was on duty to direct pedestrians and traffic as many were unsure what to do. The city Magistrate at the time, Charles Hopewell, decided on a 45 second cycle for the lights, 20 seconds for green, 5 seconds for amber and 20 seconds for red[1].

Pedestrians were said to be the biggest offenders with some ignoring the lights altogether. The police on the scene issued only warnings until people got used to the lights[2]. With the relative success of the traffic signals, more were installed downtown throughout the year[3].

City Park

When Ottawa was chosen as the capital of the Province of Canada in 1855, it was a rough lumber town with no public park. While the Parliament buildings were under construction, the city asked the Provincial government on more than one occasion to donate land on the opposite side of the Rideau Canal as a park. The government owned the land as it was once used for the homestead of Colonel John By and later his deputy, Major Daniel Bolton. However, each time they refused the city’s request as the government wished to use the land for the Governor General’s residence. Due to overrunning costs for the Parliament buildings, Rideau Hall was chosen instead.

It wasn’t until 21 August 1874 when the new Dominion government leased land across the canal from the Parliament Buildings to the city. The park became known as Major’s Hill Park, named after Bolton. The city agreed to maintain the park, build a road next to it (Mackenzie Avenue) and that the Dominion government would take back the land at any time at no cost. Due to the cost of maintaining the park, the city handed back the park to the Canadian Government in 1885[4].

Streetlights

In 1850, the first streetlamps in Ottawa (or Bytown at the time) were whale-oil lamps placed over public wells. On March 25, 1854, the Bytown Consumers Gas Company was incorporated and by the beginning of 1856, enough piping was laid to permit the lighting by gas[5]. On May 1, 1885, the first electric streetlights were switched on. The event was attended by the mayor at the time (Francis McDougal) and celebrated with a band and a parade. The Daily Citizen declared the occasion would place Ottawa ahead of any city in North America with regards to street lighting[6][7].

Powered Flight

On September 11, 1911, just 8 years after the first powered flight of the Wright Brothers, aviation came to Canada’s Capital. The Central Canada Exhibition advertised a “Famous Aviator” would perform 2 flights per day. The Exhibition was uncertain who would fly as many of their choices had dropped out. In the end, 22-year-old Lee Hammond received the honour. Hammond had just recovered from 2 crashes into water but was willing to make the flights.

With his plane, the Red Devil, he took off at 1pm from Slattery’s Field, a pasture across the Canal from the Exhibition grounds. He circled the grounds for 5 minutes and then landed safely in the field. His second flight of the day at 4pm occurred with no issues as well.

His remaining flights however did not go as smoothly. During his third flight later in the week, he encountered fog at 1,000 feet which obstructed his vision. His engine also gave out during the flight so he had to land heavily near the Canal, avoiding yet another watery crash by only a few feet.

He still attempted his second flight of the day which ended with the loss of his tail by a tree as he attempted to avoid the cows in the field. Fortunately, Hammond survived the landing. The flights were seen as a great attraction for the Exhibition, but not much was mentioned about entering a new era of transportation in Ottawa[8].

Paved Road

Macadamization came to Ottawa in June 1851 on York Street between Sussex and Dalhousie Streets. The first street to receive asphalt was Sparks Street on July 30, 1895. Mayor William Borthwick and a few aldermen were present at the corner of Sparks and Canal Streets. The mayor laid the first layer of asphalt using a shovel of polished oak and nickel plate. The mayor made a speech in which he said he was pleased and that the citizens of Ottawa would enjoy first class streets[9][10].

The first person to test the asphalt was Moses Inkerman on August 5 using his rag cart. However, onlookers jeered him off the street as it was not open to traffic yet[11].

After 3 weeks, well behind schedule and many complaints from local merchants on Sparks Street about the speed of work[12], the paving was completed. The asphalt turned out to be dangerous for horses as they would slip when the surface was wet. In one instance on November 7, 1895, the horse of Joseph Leblanc fell and broke its leg. The horse continued on but then fell again and unfortunately died[13].
