Bishop's Palace

When Ottawa was chosen as the capital of the Province of Canada in 1857, the Government needed new buildings to house the legislature and public servants. Construction contracts for all 3 buildings were initially awarded to Thomas McGreevy however he was given authority to relinquish the contracts for the East & West Blocks (Departmental Buildings) to Jones, Haycock & Co. on December 7, 1859.

Once Nepean sandstone was chosen for much of the stonework, Jones, Haycock & Co. looked to quarry the stone somewhere in Nepean Township. In 1860 and 1861, they bought parts of Lot 5 and 6, Concession 2 Ottawa Front in the township for 5 years.

Lot 6 was owned by Henry Bishop, an early settler from Ireland, like many settlers along Corkstown Road. Bishop had built a house made of the Nepean sandstone found on his property.

The quarry employed over 100 men and the work was often arduous. At the end of a long day, the workers would gather at Bishop’s house for drinks, entertainment and for some boarding. The 2-storey stone house, which became a tavern and inn, was known to many as “Bishop’s Palace”.

A century later in 1961, the National Capital Commission was in the process of expropriating properties for the Greenbelt and Lot 6 was included. Prior to purchasing the property on June 13, 1961, the NCC considered keeping Bishop’s Palace for farming use, despite needing substantial renovations. The Director of Information Division with the NCC at the time recommended the house be preserved due to its history and that it was the only house in the area to use sandstone instead of limestone.

However, by then, plans were in place to build the extension of the Queensway (Highway 417) parallel to Corkstown Road. In 1966, the NCC decided the house was beyond repair and could not be moved so they were in favour of demolition and removed the flooring for the “Canadiana Room” at 24 Sussex Drive.

News of the demolition made it to the papers but the NCC justified the demolition saying the

  • House has no important historical significance
  • Very run down and expensive to renovate
  • Very costly to move
  • Stands in the way of progress, i.e. Queensway

By the end of 1966, Bishop’s Palace was gone.
